The Housing Gap

Homeownership by race, 2005–2022 — a persistent gap that has averaged 27.1 percentage points over the period and stood at 28.2 points in 2022, when 44.1 % of Black households owned their home against 72.3 % of White households. Homes are most Americans' largest asset, so this gap is also a wealth gap.

Source: U.S. Census Bureau, American Community Survey (ACS), table B25003 (tenure by race-iteration): homeownership rate = owner-occupied households ÷ all occupied households, by race of householder, 2005–2022. 2020 is missing: the Census Bureau released no standard ACS 1-year estimates for that year, so the lines below run straight from 2019 to 2021 — the flat-looking stretch is a gap in the data, not a pause in the trend. DuBois harmonizes these public-domain data; the original Census ACS series remain authoritative.

What the data show

The Black homeownership rate has never crossed 47 % in this window, while the White rate has never fallen below 68 %, bottoming at 68.5 % in 2015. The Black–White gap averaged 27.1 percentage points across 2005–2022 and actually widened after the Great Recession: Black ownership fell from 45.8 % (2005) to a trough of 40.7 % (2016), a loss of 5.0 points, while White ownership dipped 3.3 points over the same period.

A break at 2021. The White homeownership rate steps up 2.9 points between the 2019 and 2021 ACS vintages (69.7 % to 72.6 %), far more than any other year-on-year move in the series. The 2020 experimental-weighting change sits in that gap, so comparisons that straddle it — including the widening of the gap at the end of the window — partly reflect a change in the survey rather than in housing. See Methodology & Provenance.

Because home equity is the primary store of wealth for the median American household, the homeownership gap is not separate from the wealth gap — it is one of its engines. A 27-to-28-point ownership gap compounds, decade after decade, into the 18% Black-to-White wealth ratio documented on the wealth-gap page.
