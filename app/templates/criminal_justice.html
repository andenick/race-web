Imprisonment

Imprisonment rate per 100,000 residents by race, 2010–2020 — the Bureau of Justice Statistics Prisoners series across eleven annual counts. Together with wealth, this is the harshest disparity measured on this site.

Source: Bureau of Justice Statistics, Prisoners series (2010–2020, annual). Rates are sentenced prisoners under state or federal jurisdiction per 100,000 U.S. residents of the corresponding race. DuBois reconstructs these public-domain data; the original BJS tables remain authoritative. Where the provenance lives: this series has no row in data_dictionary.csv, which covers only part of the collection. Its record sits in series_registry.json instead, as series D1011 — "Imprisonment Rate by Race", units per 100,000, source BJS Prisoners in 2020, Figure 2, years 2010–2020.

The harshest ratio

Across the eleven years from 2010 to 2020 the Black imprisonment rate averaged 5.49× the White rate. That is the joint-largest Black-to-White disparity on this site: wealth sits at 5.48× in the other direction (Black median net worth in 2022 was one-fifth of White), a statistical tie rather than a clear ordering. Both are more than twice the next-widest gaps — poverty at 2.19× and unemployment at 2.12×.

In 2010 the gap was at its widest: 1,489 Black Americans imprisoned per 100,000 versus 248 White Americans — a 6.0× ratio and an absolute gap of 1,241 per 100,000. The ratio narrowed steadily through the decade, falling to 5.08× in 2019 before a slight uptick to 5.13× in 2020, the pandemic year. (This file records imprisonment rates only; it carries no admissions or releases data, so nothing here explains what drove the 2020 level.) Even at its narrowest, a Black American was more than five times as likely to be imprisoned as a White American.
