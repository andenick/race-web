The Wealth Gap

Black median household wealth as a percentage of White median wealth, 1989–2022 — the Federal Reserve Survey of Consumer Finances across twelve triennial waves.

Source: Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System, Survey of Consumer Finances (1989–2022, triennial). Figures are inflation-adjusted median net worth by race of householder. DuBois reconstructs and harmonizes these public-domain data; the original SCF remains authoritative.

What the data show

Across the 33-year window the Black–White wealth ratio ranges from 6.0 % to 18.2 %, and it sits at or below roughly 16 % in every wave but the last. After reaching 16.3 % in 1998 it eroded through the 2000s and fell to 8.2 % in the 2013 survey — the post-Great-Recession low, and the lowest reading since 1989. Between the 2007 and 2013 waves Black median net worth fell 42.6 % against 26.9 % for White households, about 1.6 times as fast. The damage was also slower to surface: from 2007 to 2010 it was White median wealth that fell further, and the Black decline only overtook it once the 2013 wave arrived. By 2022 the ratio had rebounded to 18.2 %, the highest on record, as Black median wealth rose from $14,039 to $49,590. White median wealth over the same period rose from $170,852 to $272,000.

Even at its 2022 peak, Black median wealth is less than one fifth of White median wealth — a ratio unchanged in its essential character since the survey began in 1989.

A note on 1989. The series minimum, 6.0 % in 1989, rests on a small Black subsample and on the race coding the SCF used in that wave. We flag it as suspect and exclude it from synthesis findings, but we plot it as the survey reports it rather than quietly drop it. Read the 1989 point, and any comparison anchored on it, with that caveat in view. See Methodology & Provenance for the full note.
