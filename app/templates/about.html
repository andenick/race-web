About

Named for W.E.B. Du Bois (1868–1963), who pioneered the empirical study of race and economic stratification in America.

The namesake

In 1899, William Edward Burghardt Du Bois published The Philadelphia Negro — the first large-scale, empirically rigorous sociological study of a Black community in the United States. Working door-to-door in Philadelphia's Seventh Ward, Du Bois interviewed thousands of households, recording occupation, income, housing, family structure, and church membership. He treated race not as a slogan but as a phenomenon to be measured, and he insisted that the conditions of Black life could be understood only through systematic, disaggregated data.

That conviction — that racial stratification must be quantified to be understood — is the founding method of this dataset. We name it for him.

What this dataset is

DuBois assembles publicly-available data on racial and ethnic economic stratification in the United States across six measurable dimensions:

  • Wealth — the Federal Reserve Survey of Consumer Finances (1989–2022).
  • Income — median household income by race (Census ACS, 2005–2022).
  • Employment — unemployment by race (BLS Current Population Survey, 1972–2024 complete years; 2025 is a partial six-month average).
  • Poverty — poverty rate by race (Census ACS, 2005–2022).
  • Housing — homeownership by race (Census ACS, 2005–2022).
  • Criminal justice — imprisonment rate by race (Bureau of Justice Statistics, 2010–2020).

These are supplemented by demographic context — US total population runs 1790–2024, while the by-race breakdown is much shorter and covers only 2005–2022 — along with educational attainment, business ownership, a 2022 metro-level income cross-section, and the historical record of the trans-Atlantic slave trade (SlaveVoyages, 1514–1866).

Data, not advocacy

DuBois takes no policy position. It presents numbers, drawn from authoritative public sources, with full provenance for every figure. The gaps it documents — a Black-to-White wealth ratio that has never reached one fifth, peaking at 18.2 % in 2022, an unemployment ratio that has averaged roughly 2× for half a century — are reported as the sources record them, not as any narrative requires. Readers and researchers bring their own interpretation; the dataset's job is to make the underlying measurement honest, traceable, and reproducible.

For sources, race-category harmonization, and validation, see Methodology & Provenance. For the code that builds these series, see Code & Reproducibility. To download any dataset, see Data.
