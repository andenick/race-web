The Employment Gap

The Black-to-White unemployment ratio, 1972–2024 in complete years (2025 is a partial reading) — a stubborn roughly 2:1 line that has held across half a century of business cycles, from the Volcker recession to the COVID shock.

Source: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Current Population Survey (CPS), annual average unemployment rates by race (1972–2025). Recession shading follows NBER business-cycle dating. One caveat on the final data point: every year through 2024 averages twelve monthly observations, but 2025 averages only six (January–June), so the 2025 reading is a partial-year figure plotted alongside full-year ones and may move when the rest of the year is published. DuBois harmonizes these public-domain data; the original BLS series remain authoritative.

What the data show

Across the 53 complete years from 1972 to 2024 the Black unemployment rate has averaged 2.12× the White rate — the figure quoted elsewhere on this site, which excludes the partial 2025 reading. (Including 2025 would put it at 2.11×.) The ratio is remarkably stable: it never fell below 1.58× (2020, the COVID year, when both rates surged together) and reached as high as 2.57× in 1989. Recessions raise both rates, and the absolute gap between them usually widens in downturns — in the 1981–82 Volcker recession Black unemployment climbed to a postwar peak near 20 % while White unemployment peaked around 9.6 %.

In every single year on record a Black American was at least 1.5× as likely to be unemployed as a White American. But the shaded recession bands on the chart above hold a genuine surprise, and it runs against the intuition that downturns simply stretch the gap: the dotted ratio line falls in every one of them. It dropped from 2.34× to 2.20× across 1981–82, from 2.36× to 2.05× across 1990–91, from 2.18× to 2.07× across 2000–01, from 1.95× to 1.74× across 2008–09, and from 1.86× to 1.58× across 2019–20 — the lowest reading in the whole 54-year series.

The ratio and the gap are measuring different things, and in a slump they move in opposite directions. White unemployment rises by the larger multiple, off a much lower base, which compresses the ratio; Black unemployment rises by the larger number of percentage points, which widens the absolute gap. The COVID year is the clean case: the White rate more than doubled, from 3.3 % to 7.3 %, while the Black rate rose from 6.1 % to 11.6 % — a 1.9-fold rise. So the ratio fell to its record low of 1.58× at the very moment the gap widened from 2.8 to 4.2 percentage points. That gap-widening holds in four of those five recessions; 1990–91 is the exception, where the gap held roughly flat (6.6 to 6.4 points) before opening to 7.6 points in 1992.

The corollary is that the ratio peaks late in long expansions rather than in slumps: its two highest readings are 2.57× in 1989 and 2.48× in 1988, when the White rate had fallen to 4.5 % and 4.7 % — levels the Black rate has never once reached in this series, whose own floor is 5.5 % (2023). A tight labor market pulls the White rate down further than it ever pulls the Black rate.
