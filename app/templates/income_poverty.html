Income & Poverty

Black median household income averaged 62.8 % of White income across 2005–2022 and stood at 64.3 % in 2022, the latest year — yet Black poverty runs at 2.19× the White rate (2005–2022 average; 2.16× in 2022). Both gaps are severe, but neither approaches the wealth gap, where Black households hold less than one fifth of White median net worth.

Source: U.S. Census Bureau, American Community Survey (ACS): median household income by race (table B19013, inflation-adjusted to 2022 dollars, 2005–2022) and poverty rates by race (table B17001, 2005–2022). The ACS poverty rate is not the nation's official poverty statistic — that measure comes from the Current Population Survey's Annual Social and Economic Supplement — but it is the series with the race detail and sample size these charts need. 2020 is missing from both series: the Census Bureau released no standard ACS 1-year estimates for that year, so every line on this page steps directly from 2019 to 2021 and no COVID-year observation exists to plot. DuBois harmonizes these public-domain data; the original Census ACS series remain authoritative.

Median income: a persistent 63% ratio

From 2005 through 2022, Black median household income has stayed between 61.2 % (2015, the low) and 64.3 % (2022, the high) of White median income — in real 2022 dollars, $43,034 (2012) to $51,374 (2022) for Black households against $69,530 (2011) to $80,921 (2021) for White households. The ratio barely budged across the Great Recession and the long expansion that followed.

Note: the line joins 2019 straight to 2021. There is no 2020 point because the Census Bureau published no standard ACS 1-year estimates for 2020 — the break is a gap in the data, not a flat year.

Poverty: 2.19× the White rate

Where income is a flow, poverty is a threshold. By 2022 the Black poverty rate stood at 21.3 % against a White rate of 9.85 % — a ratio of 2.16×. Over the full series the Black poverty rate has averaged 2.19× the White rate. The gap narrowed in the late 2010s expansion but widened again in 2021.

Note: as with income, all three lines jump 2019 to 2021. No standard ACS 1-year estimates were published for 2020, so the pandemic year itself is absent from this chart.

The income gap vs the wealth gap

The contrast is the point. Black households earn about 63 cents of every dollar of White median income averaged over 2005–2022, and 64 cents in 2022, the year charted below — a large but bounded gap. Yet when the same comparison is made for wealth (net worth, accumulated across generations), Black households hold only about 18 cents of every dollar of White median wealth. Income inequality is severe; wealth inequality is an order of magnitude deeper.

The implication: closing the income gap would not, by itself, close the wealth gap. Wealth compounds across decades through homeownership, inheritance, and asset appreciation — channels the data on the pages for housing and wealth make plain.
