Historical Foundations

Two hundred and thirty-four years of population growth and three and a half centuries of the trans-Atlantic slave trade — the demographic and human-trafficking bedrock beneath every racial disparity measured on this site.

Sources: US total population 1790–2024 from MeasuringWorth (Samuel H. Williamson), cross-validated against HSUS bicentennial census reconstructions at five benchmark years (agreement averages 99.80 % and never falls below 99.70 %, its 1850 low). Trans-Atlantic slave-trade data from the SlaveVoyages Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database (TAST 2019 release). Both are public-domain; DuBois reconstructs them faithfully.

{# ───────────────────────── Chart 1: US population 1790–2024 ───────────────────────── #}

A nation's growth, 1790–2024

From fewer than 4 million at the first census to over 340 million today, US population grew roughly 86-fold in 234 years — an average of about 1.4 million people per year across the span, with the most rapid percentage growth in the antebellum decades.

{# ──────────────────────── Chart 2: Slave trade 1514–1866 ──────────────────────── #}

The trans-Atlantic slave trade, 1514–1866

The SlaveVoyages database documents {{ '{:,}'.format(summary.total_voyages | int) }} voyages that embarked {{ '{:,.0f}'.format(summary.total_embarked_imputed | float) }} enslaved Africans. An estimated 13.7 % died during the Middle Passage — roughly 1.46 million people — leaving {{ '{:,.0f}'.format(summary.total_disembarked_imputed | float) }} to disembark in the Americas. The record runs from the first documented voyage in 1514 to the final three voyages in 1866, a year after the legal abolition of slavery in the United States — 352 years end to end, though only 335 of those 353 calendar years carry a recorded voyage. The 18 blank years all fall between 1515 and 1543, where the surviving record is thinnest.

Each bar = total enslaved persons who disembarked in the Americas that year (TAST 2019, imputed). Arrivals peaked in the 1820s (733,525), ahead of the 1800s (703,254) and the 1790s (701,818); the heaviest eighteenth-century decade, the 1790s, ranks third.

What the data show

The two series bookend the economic history this site examines. The population curve traces a young republic's expansion through immigration, territorial acquisition, and natural increase. The slave-trade curve traces the forced migration that, over three and a half centuries, carried {{ '{:,.0f}'.format(summary.total_embarked_imputed | float) }} Africans out of Africa and landed {{ '{:,.0f}'.format(summary.total_disembarked_imputed | float) }} of them alive in the Americas — the foundational labor system of the Atlantic economy.

Where those arrivals landed is a question this dataset answers only in part. The region-level file (slavetrade_by_region.csv) codes 10,367 of the 36,108 voyages and 2,891,050 of the 9,202,995 arrivals — about 29 % of voyages and 31 % of people. Every figure in it is therefore a subset, not a national total, and it cannot be read as a count of arrivals in mainland North America. Within the coded subset, 86.5 % of arrivals are recorded in Brazil and the Caribbean.

Together, these are the denominator and the dark numerator of American economic stratification: the population that grew, and the people who were trafficked to build that growth.
