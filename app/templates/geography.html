The Geography of the Gap

Black and White median household income across {{ metro_count }} US metro and micro areas, 2022 — the widest gaps track low Black incomes far more closely than high White ones, and a majority of them sit in the Midwest.

Source: US Census Bureau, American Community Survey — median household income by race of householder, tables B19013A (White alone) and B19013B (Black or African American alone), 2022. One White-alone and one Black-alone median is shown for each metropolitan and micropolitan statistical area. The sources on file record the survey and the year but not the estimate vintage, so none is asserted here. Where the provenance lives: this series has no row in data_dictionary.csv, which covers only part of the collection. Its record sits in series_registry.json instead, as series D1013 — "Median Income by Race by Metro", units current USD, source Census ACS B19013A/B, year 2022. DuBois reconstructs these public-domain data; the original ACS tables remain authoritative.

What the data show

The three widest gaps are the ones you would guess. In San Jose–Sunnyvale–Santa Clara, White median household income reaches $152,753 while Black median income is just $71,070 — a gap of $81,683, the largest in the country. Nearby Santa Cruz–Watsonville is second, where the Black-to-White ratio falls to 0.30× on a $78,339 gap, and San Francisco–Oakland–Berkeley is third. But those three are the entirety of California's presence in the top fifteen, and the rest of the list looks nothing like them.

Across all {{ metro_count }} areas the dollar gap correlates only moderately with White income (+0.39) and far more strongly, and negatively, with Black income (−0.62). What separates a wide-gap metro from a narrow one is mostly how little the Black median reaches, not how high the White median climbs. Among the fifteen widest gaps the median White income is $79,956 — barely above the $70,230 median across all {{ metro_count }} areas — while the median Black income is $27,972, against $44,621 across all areas. La Crosse–Onalaska, Wisconsin, ranks seventh on a White median of only $71,586; what puts it there is a Black median of $17,927.

The regional pattern is not the coastal one either. Sorting the fifteen widest dollar gaps by Census region gives Midwest 8 — Faribault–Northfield MN, Jefferson City MO, La Crosse–Onalaska WI, Kankakee IL, Springfield IL, Dubuque IA, Green Bay WI and Iowa City IA — against West 4 (the three California metros plus Flagstaff AZ), South 2 (Charlottesville VA, Jefferson GA) and Northeast 1 (Trenton–Princeton NJ). No Pacific Northwest area appears at all. A clear majority of the country's widest Black–White income gaps sit in mid-sized Midwestern metros with ordinary White incomes and very low Black ones.
